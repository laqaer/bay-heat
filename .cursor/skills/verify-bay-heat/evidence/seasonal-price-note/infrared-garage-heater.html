Radiant (infrared/quartz) heat warms people and surfaces directly, so it still feels warm right where the door is open or a draft cuts across the floor. Forced-air heats the room's air, which a big door opening or a leaky envelope keeps flushing outside. A drafty shop or a bay with the door up often does better on radiant; a fully enclosed garage that needs even heat throughout does better on forced-air.

Why the door matters more than the wattage

A forced-air heater — a 240V fan-forced unit or a vented gas unit heater — raises the temperature of the air in the room, then relies on that warm air staying put. Where radiant alone falls short

  • Why not a 240V quartz/tube infrared ceiling heater (3-6kW)? A fully enclosed, occupied garage that needs even heat throughout — a finished workshop you sit in for hours — is better served by forced-air or a mini-split. Radiant only warms what's in its direct line of sight; the far corner of a closed room stays cold under a spot infrared element.

Radiant panels also do nothing for the air itself, so a garage that needs to keep tools, paint, or stored goods above a minimum temperature — not just a person working in it — needs forced-air or a heat pump instead.

Safety

Both classes are electric resistance, not combustion, but a radiant element runs hot to the touch and aims heat outward on purpose. Keep the manual's clearance from anything flammable, and never aim one at a vehicle's fuel fill or a gasoline can. A 120V unit still needs GFCI protection on the outlet it plugs into (A code or reference citationNFPA 70, National Electrical Code, 2023 · checked 2026-09-25Report a problem). Your electrician and your local code edition govern.
